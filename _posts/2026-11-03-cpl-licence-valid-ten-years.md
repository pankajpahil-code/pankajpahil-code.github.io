Many sets of notes still handed around say it: **CPL, five years. ATPL, two.**

Both are out of date. The ATPL already read five years before 2023, and since October 2023 the CPL
and the ATPL are both ten.

## What changed

The Aircraft (First Amendment) Rules, 2023, notified as **G.S.R. 733(E) on 10 October 2023**,
amended the table under **Rule 39C of the Aircraft Rules, 1937**. Against the Airline Transport
Pilot's Licence and the Commercial Pilot's Licence, "Five years" was replaced by "Ten years".

The Student Pilot's Licence had moved a year and a half earlier. **G.S.R. 289(E) of 8 April
2022** replaced "Five years" with "Ten years" in its row of the same table.

| Licence | Validity |
|---|---|
| ATPL | **Ten years** (since October 2023) |
| CPL | **Ten years** (since October 2023) |
| PPL | Ten years |
| Student Pilot's Licence | **Ten years** (since April 2022) |

## Two things that are not licence validity

**Your medical has its own validity.** A Class 1 medical runs for 12 months, and for less in some
cases, such as pilots over 40 flying single-pilot commercial operations. Check the current DGCA
medical CAR for your own case rather than a figure in old notes.

**The Instrument Rating is a rating, not a licence.** It is valid for 12 months and is not
affected by the changes above. If a question asks about IR validity and you answer with a licence
figure, you have answered a different question.

## Why old notes still say five

Because five was right for a long time, and notes get photocopied more often than they get
rewritten. Notes typed before these amendments are not dishonest. They are out of date, and they
are still going round the study groups.

Read the amendments themselves, G.S.R. 289(E) of 8 April 2022 and G.S.R. 733(E) of 10 October
2023, not a consolidated copy of the Rules. Many copies in circulation predate them and still
print the old figures.

## For the exam

> **ATPL, CPL, PPL and SPL: ten years. Instrument Rating: 12 months, and it is a rating.**
